# Embroidery engine (image to stitch file)

Pure JavaScript that turns a picture (a client's logo, text) into a machine embroidery file. It has no React, no DOM and
no Node-only calls, so the same code runs in the page, in a Web Worker and under plain `node` (that is how it is tested).
The page that uses it is `src/pages/Digitizer.jsx` (route `/digitizer`, Operations menu).

## Units and coordinates (read this first, every module obeys it)

- **Design space** is millimetres, floats, origin at the hoop centre, **+x right, +y DOWN** (screen convention).
- **Pattern space** (what a file holds) is **integer units of 0.1 mm**, +y down, **absolute** positions.
  The writers flip y themselves where a format wants y up (DST does, as pyembroidery does).
- Angles are degrees, 0 = along +x, positive turns clockwise on screen (because y is down).
- Image pixels are in the picture's own pixel grid (floats allowed), +y down. `mmPerPx` converts to design space.

## The pattern (`pattern.js`)

```js
{ name: "design",
  threads: [{ r, g, b, name?, code?, brand? }],        // one per colour block, in sewing order
  stitches: [[x, y, cmd], ...] }                       // absolute, 0.1 mm integers
CMD = { STITCH: 0, JUMP: 1, TRIM: 2, COLOR_CHANGE: 3, STOP: 4, END: 5 }
```

Same meaning as pyembroidery's `EmbPattern`: a JUMP moves without sewing, a TRIM cuts the thread (at the current
position), a COLOR_CHANGE asks for the next thread (at the current position, no movement), END closes the file.
A pattern with N colour blocks has N threads and N-1 COLOR_CHANGE commands.

## Rules

- Plain ES modules, JSDoc comments in plain language, double quotes, semicolons, 2 spaces (match `src/lib/*.js`).
- No imports from React, `window`, `document`, `fs`, `Buffer`. Bytes are `Uint8Array` / `DataView`. Text is ASCII by hand.
- The only dependency allowed is `clipper-lib` (geometry only). Nothing from `kazi mobile/`.
- **Licences.** pyembroidery (MIT) and libembroidery (zlib) may be ported with a credit in the file header.
  **Ink/Stitch is GPL-3 and this repo is public: never copy or transliterate its code, only learn ideas from its
  documentation and behaviour.** PEmbroider's licence is checked per file before anything is ported.
- Every module is testable with `node` alone (no browser). Heavy loops use typed arrays.

**What the stitch engine decides (`stitch/engine.js`).** Each island of a shape is tried as satin and kept only if it is a steady stroke (for "Auto": at most 5 mm wide, at least 3.5 times longer than wide, no part more than 2.2 times its average width) whose stitches would cover it (85%, thread taken as 0.45 mm), not spill past its edge, not pile four threads on more than 20% of it, not lay more than its share of thread, and not leave more than 1.3 mm between neighbouring needle holes on an edge (0.8 mm when the engine chooses by itself, `autoSatinGap`: the holes of one edge are 0.6 mm apart on a straight strip, so a column whose edge spreads past 0.8 is a fan with cloth showing between its stitches, and a fill covers that shape better; on the 130 test pictures at 100 mm the satin islands went from 8068 to 1840, the colour error fell 2.6 % and the error in the fine detail 4.2 %, and at 1.3 on the 129 pictures of the last check the colour error rose 0.19, the fine detail 0.38 and 4.6 times as many islands went to satin; satin asked for by hand keeps the 1.3 mm of `satinGap`; typed lettering, an object of kind "text", is held to 2 mm, `textSatinGap`, because the letters of a font are strokes for which satin is the right stitch and 1.3 sent the bold letters K, Z and I, which pass every other check, to a ragged fill); otherwise it is filled with tatami and `generate()` says which check refused satin in `info` (`satin refused: needle holes 2.4 mm apart on an edge (up to 2)`). Parts thinner than 0.4 mm (`satinMinStroke`) are left out of a satin layout. A satin column's rows are 0.3 mm apart (a fill's 0.4): the holes on one edge are twice the row spacing apart, and at 0.4 cloth shows in the notches of narrow lettering. The separate pieces of one shape (the letters of a word) are sewn in a planned order, nearest first and then untangled, so there is no long hop at the end. Colours are grouped to save colour changes only where nothing overlaps; the design's own order decides which colour comes first. Jumps up to 3 mm are not cut; longer ones are tied off, trimmed, and tied in. Pieces too small to sew are named in ONE warning, and the crowded-spot check (`stitch/checks.js`) flags 15 or more needle holes in a millimetre and a half square (the lock stitches of every start and end are a dozen at most). **Small pieces.** A piece down to 0.3 mm2 is sewn (it was 0.6) the way a digitizer would: no growth past its edge (`grow` is 0), a pull allowance that shrinks with its width (0.45 to 1 times the usual), rows that stop 0.07 to 0.15 mm inside the edge (a thread is 0.4 mm wide and its end is round, so a row that stops AT the edge looks 0.2 mm past it, and the lobes of a leaf and the gap between two leaves close up), and, for a piece under 40 mm2 that is 1.6 times longer than wide, rows across it (the short way, like a satin) unless the person chose an angle. **Speed.** The order of overlapping shapes is found by drawing every shape into one 0.1 mm grid (cutting every pair with the polygon engine took minutes; a design too wide for that grid falls back to `overlappingPairs` in `stitch/overlap.js`, which finds the same pairs on the shapes' own grid, and only a small design is cut pair by pair), a satin ray looks only at the grid cells it passes through, a shape of over 4000 points is not tried as satin, and a field cut by hundreds of holes (over 5000 points: the paper of an engraving) is sewn with cheap geometry (no edge underlay, no offsets, a fast walk along its outline). An engraving at 250 mm went from 300 s to 5 to 25 s with the same stitches. One island the satin layout cannot make sense of is filled instead and no longer stops the whole design. **Lines.** A long piece thinner than `lineMaxWidth` (0.58 mm) and at least `lineRatio` (2.5) times longer than wide (`lineHoles`: also one with holes, the loops of an a, an e, an o or a network of thin lines, as long as its middle lines are no wider than `lineWide` (1.2) times that on average: the black of an engraving, a mass full of tiny holes, is not a network of lines) is sewn as ONE line along the middle of its skeleton (`strokeCenterlines`): each stretch of `lineLen` (1.4 mm) forward, back and forward again (a bean stitch, one bold thread about half a millimetre wide), the branches of a shape joined where they meet. An island is sewn by this alone, never also as satin or tatami. `lineImpl` chooses how the line is laid: "runs" (`lineRuns`, the skeleton's chains as they are, used) or "island" (`lineIsland` in `stitch/satin.js`: strokes that run straight through a junction are one line, the ends run on to the tip of the shape, the stitches follow a curve, `curveRun` in `stitch/path.js`); on the 129 pictures at 100 mm the two scored the same (colour error 6.68 and 6.66, thin lines found 0.631 and 0.633) and "island" made 36 more stitches under 0.3 mm a picture and 1.7 % more stitches, so "runs" is the default. **Thin strokes that grow out of a piece** (`thinArms`): a smile that touches a nose, a hairline serif, a whisker on a nose belong to ONE island with the body they grow out of, and the rows of a fill do not fit in a stroke under 0.58 mm, so they were lost (the smile of a cartoon panda was gone, its line recall 0.96 to 0.60 once the strokes were no longer thickened to 0.8 mm). The island is opened with a disc of half `lineMaxWidth` (shrunk, then grown by that much); what the disc cannot enter and is at least `thinArmMin` (1.8 mm) long, 0.15 to 0.58 mm wide and meets the piece only at its root (about its own width: a sliver along the edge of a ragged outline meets it along its whole length) is cut away and sewn as a line after the piece, which is filled without it. Only a piece whose perimeter squared is at least `thinArmsRatio` (40) times its area and that has at most `thinArmsVerts` (500) points is looked at (cutting the big ragged regions of a photograph cost as much time as the rest of the picture and found next to nothing: no change in the errors at 500 points). On the corpus at 100 mm the panda's line recall is 0.88 again, the clip-art woman's colour error falls 11 % and the Bach monogram's 12 %. A satin column leaves out the parts of a stroke thinner than `satinMinStroke` (0.4 mm, also `minStroke` in `SATIN_DEFAULTS`; at 0.6 the thin strokes of small lettering broke into dashes). **Moves between runs (`stitch/engine.js` `buildCover`, `plan.js`).** A jump of more than `trimOver` (3 mm) is a thread cut on a machine, which takes seconds and ties the thread off and on again (six stitches), so a move that would lie under what is sewn LATER is stitched instead (`hiddenTravel`, up to `travelMax` 25 mm, a stitch every `travelStep` 2.5 mm): the engine draws every fill into a coarse grid in sewing order, so each cell knows which item covers it last, and a thread laid from the needle to the next run is hidden when every cell along it (and its neighbours) is covered by a later item or by a patch of its own colour. On the corpus at 100 mm this takes 13 % of the thread cuts and 14 % of the jumps away, the stitches go down (fewer tie stitches), and the rendered stitches stay the same. A better order does not help: the best possible order inside each colour (a travelling-salesman search over the engine's own entry and exit points, no overlap rules) leaves the number of moves over 3 mm where it is (169 to 171, 624 to 679, 99 to 97 in three pictures) and shortens the total by 5 to 18 %. What cuts more is a longer `trimOver`: 5 mm takes the cuts down by 30 %, 7 mm by 52 %, 10 mm by 70 %, at the price of loose thread floating over the cloth between the pieces. **Needle holes (`stitch/plan.js`, `path.js`, `fill.js`).** A thread piles up in a hole the needle drops into twice in a row, and a machine file is only as good as its shortest stitches, so: a thread starts with a jump to a point `startLead` (0.4 mm) along the run and a stitch back onto its first point (a jump followed by a stitch of no length put a zero-length record in the file for every jump, about 3 % of all the records); the tie stitches go towards a point at least 0.6 mm along the run, and a run shorter than that gets none; a running stitch (`runAlong`) makes no hole within `minGap` (0.5 mm) of the one before it, so the many tiny corners of a traced curve are not a stitch a tenth of a millimetre long each; the walk onto the outline to run along it starts at the outline's own first corner instead of hopping a quarter of a millimetre onto it; and in the end every stitch shorter than `shortMm` (0.3 mm) is merged into its neighbour when the path through its neighbours passes within `shortTol` (0.05 mm) of the hole and the merged stitch is no longer than `shortMax` (7 mm); corners, tie stitches and the first and last stitch of a run stay. On the corpus (130 real pictures at 100 mm): zero-length stitch records 527 -> 0 a picture, stitches under 0.3 mm 724 -> 45, stitches 17991 -> 16348, crowded spots 268 -> 181, the rendered stitches the same to the eye (mean difference 0.4 of 255).

**How a picture becomes shapes (`trace/`, `src/lib/digitizer/convert.js`).** (1) The picture's kind is found from how well 16 colours describe its FLAT pixels, on a lightly blurred copy, so edges, lettering and JPEG noise do not count (`trace/photo.js`): a logo (flat colours, however many up to 16), artwork with soft shading, or a photograph. An engraving or a woodcut (black ink on paper, so a few flat colours describe it) has its lines closer together than a thread can follow, and sewn as a logo it comes out a mass of scribbles; on a grid of 0.15 mm it has strong local contrast (a 3 x 3 neighbourhood that spans more than 60 of 255) at half of its pixels, so a picture with 500 or more pixels on its long side, up to `hatchedMaxMm` (120 mm) wide, that has `hatched` (0.5) of them is sewn as a photograph, which makes the tone the lines add up to (colour error at 100 mm: the lion engraving -7.0, the Droeshout Shakespeare -4.4, the Yggdrasil bookplate -4.0, the Duerer woodcut -2.5, the Dore engraving -1.7; at 60 mm the lion -11.3 and the Yggdrasil -12.7); a bigger design can follow the lines, and stays a drawing. A photograph is first flattened (Kuwahara filter) and traced on a grid of about 0.15 mm (a coarser one lost twigs, lashes and small petals; the pixels the flattening changed most, at most the strongest 3.5 %, are put back: lashes, whiskers, hairs, twigs); the colours of artwork and photographs are merged more (delta-E 8 and 6 against 11 for a logo). A small picture that is enlarged for tracing (its pixels would be bigger than 0.4 mm once stitched) finds its colours on its own pixels, not on the enlarged copy, so the colours do not change with the size it is sewn at (before, the real sun picture gave 7, 8 or 9 colours at neighbouring sizes and the rabbit 6 colours up to 0.4 mm a pixel and 8 above it; self-test section 10; a picture that is not enlarged, so any size up to 0.4 mm a pixel, traces exactly as before). Whatever the kind, the grid is never finer than 0.06 mm a pixel (a small design is traced on a smaller copy of a big picture: the widening below costs time with the cube of the pixels a millimetre). (2) A background is removed only when it is plain (a photograph's sky stays, and sets the cloth colour). (3) Thin strokes are kept, not cleaned away: lettering and hairlines of the minor colours, and any thin letter that stands by itself inside the picture (white lettering on a dark badge), are widened to about half a millimetre along their own middle lines (`thickenMm`; the default follows the detail slider, 0.8 mm at the simplest, 0.6 at Balanced, 0.5 at the default detail and 0.45 at the finest: a millimetre filled the counters of small lettering and made the hairlines of an engraving heavy bars; a stroke already as wide is left as it is, and the disc that widens a stroke is `floor(r)` pixels, so a line of thread comes out 0.5 mm and not 0.7 (`thickenKept` in `trace/labels.js`: 129 pictures at 100 mm, colour error 6.73 to 6.68, 1 % fewer stitches); in a photograph or artwork only pieces that are long and thin count, not patches of colour). The widened pixels are marked (`PAINTED`) so they are not counted when the colour is measured; a colour that is only thin strokes takes its ink from the far end of what it holds, not the washed-out mean. Ink that is only thin lines never makes a flat-pixel cluster, so `missingColours` looks for pixels that no palette colour and no mix of two explains, beside only one palette colour (the halo of JPEG between two regions is beside two), and adds that colour. (4) Every shape reaches 0.4 mm under the shapes sewn after it, so a gap in the stitching or the seam between two colours shows thread, never cloth. It reaches only where a later shape really lies over the growth: a shape inside the hole of a later border (a ring) does not grow. The box cut that keeps this quick (`clipToBox` in `convert.js`) once read the hole of such a border as covering the shape, and every shape inside the border grew 0.4 mm all round: the lettering of a seal doubled in weight (the FBI seal at 250 mm: colour error 10.1 to 6.0, at 100 mm 10.6 to 6.3). (5) A dark or coloured background means the picture was made for that garment, so it becomes the cloth colour; a light one leaves the cream default. A colour in the picture that is like the cloth is not stitched where it is wide (the cloth shows through), but its shapes narrower than 2.5 mm (`CLOTH_FINE_MM` in `digitizer/convert.js`) are stitched in their own colour over the shape round them where they are ink: white lettering or a thin white ring inside a navy badge cannot be kept clean as a gap in a fill. "Ink" means the shape sits in a hole of a wide shape (4 mm or more, `CLOTH_FIELD_MM`; touching shapes share exact boundary points, which is how the hole it sits in is found). The white inside a letter (its counter: the hole of an a, e, B or D) sits in the hole of a narrow shape, and a white outline round the whole design sits in no hole, and both stay cloth. This is for logos and for the page's own colour; in a portrait or photograph a narrow patch of a tone near the cloth's (the folds of a dark coat on the dark cloth the picture's background made) is a detail of the picture and is stitched. The palette entry then reads "fine parts only" (`fineOnly`); on the 129 pictures this rule costs 2.8 % more stitches (one crest 60 %) and takes the colour error 0.04 down. A change of cloth colour can turn a colour from "fine parts only" into all of it without it ever being switched off, so the colours that changed are found by ONE function, `changedColours` in `digitizer/convert.js`, which `objectsFromTrace`, `mergeTraceObjects` and the worker's shapes answer (`digitizer/engineCore.js`) all use (the worker once looked only at on and off, sent no shapes for such a colour, and the page took them out of the design; `trace/selftest.mjs` asks the worker for them). (6) A new picture is made as big as the hoop allows (what is stitched, not the margins), then traced again at that size. The thread colours are the picture's own (the chart name is only a label unless "Use the nearest thread colours" is on). How big a small piece must be depends on how much it stands out (`contrast`): the usual limit is scaled by (24 / the colour distance to its neighbours) to the power 1.25, between 0.3 mm2 and three times the limit, so a bright petal on dark leaves stays and a patch of sky a shade off goes; and a thin piece with ONE colour on both its sides that is 26 or more apart from it (a vein, a twig, a lash, the gap between two leaves) is kept and widened to `strongMm` (a vein, a twig or a lash: 0.5 mm up to a 60 mm design, 0.3 mm from 100 mm, which is less than the thread but a bean line is wider than the thread anyway; a bigger design has bigger lines, and a wide painting overdraws them) or `gapMm` (a gap or a dot, 0.7 mm, never wider than the strokes' own width), lettering to `thickenMm` (0.5 mm by default). **Thin lines of artwork and photographs** (`lines`, `thinLines` in `trace/photo.js`): a lash, a whisker, a hair, a vein or a twig is found straight from the luminance of the unflattened picture, because the colour patches have long since lost it. A dark line is what a closing of the luminance fills in, a light one (only on a dark ground) what an opening takes away; a piece counts when it stands out by more than `lineTau` (40 of 255) and is long and thin (`lineMm`, 1.2 mm), and all lines together are held to the strongest `lineShare` of the picture (3 % up to 100 mm, falling with the size to 1.2 % at 250 mm: the bigger the design, the more of a picture's texture reaches the size of a line, and a line is never thinner than a thread). Each line takes the palette colour of the same hue that is nearest its own (never a more colourful one, nor one lighter than a dark line by more than 10 L) and is painted onto the labels before the cleaning, so the cleaning cannot take it away; the engine sews it as a line (see above). A picture is held to about `maxShapes` (2500; up to 2.4 times as many from a 240 mm design: the small leaves, petals and lashes of a big picture) pieces by raising the limits (feathers and bark at full detail were 10000 pieces and minutes of work). A picture whose pixels would be bigger than 0.4 mm in the design (a 64 pixel icon in a 100 mm patch) is enlarged smoothly first, to about 0.2 mm a pixel. **Detail by kind.** The slider can mean more for a portrait, artwork or a photograph than for a logo (`trace/detail.js`, `limitsFor`: read half a step higher, because their small shapes are the eyes, brows and wrinkles); the tracer does so when it is given the slider's value as `detail`, and the page gives it only when `KIND_AWARE_DETAIL` (`digitizer/convert.js`) is true. It is OFF: the default 0.75 was tuned for every kind alike. On the 129 pictures at 100 mm ON gives small regions kept 55.7 to 59.5 %, colour error 6.68 to 6.52, fine detail 8.16 to 7.92 and thin lines found 0.63 to 0.67, for 9.7 % more stitches, 20 % more thread cuts and 33 % more jumps (faces +7.3 points for 10 % more stitches, trees +4.9 for 13 %, line art +7.9 for 22 %, logos unchanged); the Finest step of the slider does the same for one picture on request. What is still hard: lettering under about 4 mm tall (the page warns under 3.5 mm), and hairline calligraphy, whose strokes are thinner than any needle.

**How the shapes are sewn (`src/lib/digitizer/look.js`; `node src/lib/digitizer/selftest.mjs` checks it).** Two settings, applied when the converter makes the objects (`traceOptions` carries `look`, the tracer's answer keeps it, `objectsFromTrace` applies it; a hand-set angle, hand-traced shapes and text are never touched). *Angles* (`angles.js`, ON): a shape clearly longer than wide (1.6 times, the engine's own limit for small pieces) has its rows run along its length, and touching shapes (they share exact boundary points) take their angles from eight (45, 135, 90, 0 ...) so that neighbours differ by 40 degrees or more; the angles are worked out over every shape of the picture together, so switching a colour off and on never changes the angle of a shape that stays. Measured on 25 real pictures at 100 mm (faces, trees, leaves, animals, flowers, a crest): 6.2 % fewer stitches, about 40 fewer crowded spots and 18 fewer stitches under 0.3 mm a picture, 1 % more thread cuts, colour and fine-detail error unchanged (rows along a long shape are long, so there are fewer row ends). Rows ACROSS a long shape, like a satin, cost more and looked no better; following the grain of the picture under a shape (the structure tensor of its lightness) gave a detail-error gain of 0.016 and 1.3 fewer cuts and no visible change, so it was dropped. On flat logos the edges come out the same on average (bold lettering a little more ragged, a bold flag smoother). *Edges* (`edges.js`, ON for logos and artwork, never for photographs): a line of running stitch (0.4 mm thread, 3 mm stitches, its middle 0.2 mm inside the true edge: `inset` in `stitch/outline.js`) round every big flat shape (mean width 3 mm and area 10 mm² or more, not a stroke that may be sewn as satin), with the fill's rows stopping 0.3 mm short of the edge under it (a row end is round and 0.4 mm wide and would stick out past the line). Without it the sewn shapes come out about 0.17 mm FATTER than the traced ones all round (a row end reaches the edge and the thread is wider than its end), which closes the little gaps and counters of lettering; with it they are 0.05 mm fatter, and the teeth of a straight edge sewn at 45 degrees (a third of a millimetre deep) are covered. On the 9 of 18 flat real pictures that get a line (a bold wordmark, cartoons, flags, a seal, big black teeth), against the same pictures with rows only: 3.4 % more stitches, 1.7 more thread cuts, 6.9 more crowded spots, colour error 0.39 lower, fine-detail error 0.74 lower, thin lines found +0.06 (on the biggest gains, colour error 2.42 to 1.45 and fine-detail error 5.70 to 3.22; a wordmark's thin-line match 0.27 to 0.56). Judged on the screen only: the preview draws thread 0.45 mm wide against a real 0.38 mm, so part of that gain is the preview's own fatness, and pull on real cloth may move the edge either way. A bean line (three passes) cost 23 % more stitches and 90 more crowded spots for no gain; stitches of 2 mm instead of 3 gave a little more gain for 1.7 % more stitches. The line is on top of up to 20 more crowded spots on a picture that already has hundreds (a seal with fine lettering). Nothing here has been sewn.

**The fine details on top (`trace/details.js`, `trace/fine.js`, `fineObjects` in `digitizer/convert.js`).** A picture turned into flat patches of a few colours loses what is thinner or smaller than a patch: a leaf's veins, twigs against the sky, the lid and lashes of an eye, hair, a pupil, a flower's centre. For photographs and artwork (never a logo: the tracer keeps those exactly) they are found directly on the picture, not on the colour layer: `extractDetails` (`details.js`) takes the picture itself (at most 1600 px on its long side, not the tracer's coarser work copy), finds ridges of lightness at three scales from the second derivatives of the Gaussian-smoothed picture with a sub-pixel centre (so the edge of a patch is not taken for a line), links them into polylines, scores each by how much its centre differs from both its sides in CIELAB, and keeps the strongest and longest within a budget of millimetres of line per square centimetre (more at a higher Detail setting; the numbers are `detailParams`); small compact blobs of 0.45 to 8 mm² are outlined as dots. Everything physical is in millimetres, so the same picture gives the same details at any size. `fine.js` then checks them against the colour layer the tracer made: a line or a dot that a thin or small shape of the colour layer already carries is not added a second time (a line that is partly carried keeps only its free stretches, at least 1.5 mm), and each one records which colour of the colour layer lies under it. The converter (`fineObjects`) makes the objects: one object of open lines for each thread (`open: true`, no fill, a bean stroke 0.45 mm; a narrow satin column of 0.7 mm when thicker lines are asked for, because one thread over a dense fill can sink into it) and one of small dots for each thread, all `source: "image"`, `layer: "detail"`, after every shape of the colour layer. Colours: each detail is sewn in the thread of the colour layer nearest its own (within 18 CIELAB), so no new needle; when none is near and the machine has needles to spare (`maxColors`), up to two new threads are added; at most three threads in all, so the end of the sew has at most three more colour changes; a detail that would not show on what lies under it (less than 14 CIELAB different) is left out. Amount: their stitches are kept under 30 % of the colour layer's, and their separate pieces (each may cost a thread cut) under a fifth of its shapes and at least 12 (the weakest and shortest go first). The stitch engine sews them last (`scheduleOrder` holds a detail item back until everything before it in the design is sewn, whatever the colour saving would like), dots before lines, dark threads before light ones; lines are ordered nearest end first and untangled (2-opt), a hop under the trim distance is a jump and a longer one a trim. The page asks the worker for them with the trace (`details` option, `fineDetail` = the Detail slider) and shows what came of them ("Added N fine lines and M dots"); the Picture panel's "Fine details on top" switches the layer off or on.

**What the fine details come to, and what was tried and left out.** The layer as shipped (added on top of the colour layer, checked against it), details on against off, over 85 real photographs and artworks at 100 mm: line match +0.005 (of 0.59), error in the fine detail unchanged (-0.002), colour error +0.010 (of 7.6), +0.5 % stitches, +1.1 % thread cuts, one more colour change, about half a second more to read the picture; better on 21 pictures, worse on 11 (by 0.01 to 0.05 in line match: there recall is the same or a little higher and precision lower, because the lines added are faint ones, skin and hair strands and pale veins, with a contrast from about 9 CIELAB up, while the ruler only counts a thin line of the source when it stands out by more than 18 lightness steps, so they are charged against precision and never credited); logos identical on all 53. At most 6.8 % more stitches and 9.2 % more thread cuts on any one picture (a woodcut), and the separate pieces are held to a fifth of the colour layer's shapes (never reached on these pictures). At 200 mm (12 pictures): line match +0.006, error in the fine detail -0.015, +0.8 % stitches, +2.5 % thread cuts, two more colour changes. The experiments below were measured on 42 of those pictures at 100 mm. Letting the colour layer stop looking for thin lines of its own and leaving them all to the detail layer (`replaceLines`): line match -0.043 (the colour layer's finder is the more inclusive one), so it stays. Keeping two of the machine's colours back from the colour layer for threads of the details' own colours: line match +0.014 but colour error +0.19 and detail error +0.16, so no. A stricter detector (contrast x1.4 or x1.8): line match and colour error within 0.001 of the shipped result, error in the fine detail 0.002 to 0.006 worse, fewer lines (8 a picture, 6 at x1.8) and fewer pictures worse (5 of 42, 3 at x1.8), but also fewer faint veins and twigs (the peepal tree's gain falls from +0.049 to +0.013), so the shipped setting stays; a looser colour tolerance (26 instead of 18): within 0.005. Judged by eye, the gain is largest on engravings and woodcuts (Duerer's rhinoceros: line match +0.098, error in the fine detail -0.62) and on trees, and small on faces, where the colour layer already carries most lashes, lids and brows.

**A frame, and three things the detail layer was throwing away (2026-11-09, found on `test.jpg`, a 600 px dragon and tiger stitch chart with a 1 to 2 pixel black frame round it).** (1) *The frame (`trace/frame.js`, option `trimFrame`, ON; the answer's `stats.frame` says how thick it was).* The frame was the one colour touching the whole border, so it was the BACKGROUND: the page made the cloth black (#010101) and every black outline, whisker and pupil of the picture was left unsewn as "like the cloth", while the white paper inside the frame was sewn as a 100 x 100 mm fill (34,538 stitches at 100 mm; 31,282 with the frame taken off, and the outlines back). `removeFrame` paints a frame of 1 to 10 pixels (at most 1.6 % of the short side) over with the pixels just inside it, only when the outer ring is ONE colour all the way round and the band just inside is a plain margin (75 % one colour) of a lightness at least 70 of 255 different; a design that fills its picture, a thick dark border or a see-through edge is left alone. The picture keeps its size. (2) *The halo rule (`HALO_RATIO` in `trace/details.js`).* A ridge was dropped as the "halo" of an opposite-sort ridge when that was at least 0.8 times as strong, which also threw away the STRONGER of two neighbours; the dark whiskers of the tiger and the pale gaps between them were each taken for the halo of the other and both went (six candidate lines of dE 32 to 59 in the whisker area). It is now 1.25 times. (3) *The budget was spent before the carried check (`OVERFETCH`, `CARRY_DE` in `trace/fine.js`).* The finder kept its best 22 lines for an artwork (the budget for artwork is half a photograph's), all long outlines the colour layer already sews, `fine.js` then dropped every one as "carried", and the lines nothing else had never got in. The finder is now asked for 3 times its budget, what the colour layer carries is dropped, and then the usual budget is applied; and a small shape only carries a detail of about its own colour (`CARRY_DE`, 26 CIELAB then, 18 now, see the next paragraph: a dark whisker over an orange patch is not carried by the patch; before, a small shape of ANY colour carried it, and a picture of thousands of small patches lost nearly every fine line). On `test.jpg` at 100 mm the finder gives 116 lines instead of 22, 96 are carried by the colour layer, 20 are kept and 5 to 7 sewn (the colour layer carries most of what is fine here; the rest is the weakest). Six other pictures (a logo, a cartoon, a photograph, a gradient, the Kazi logo, drawn lettering) give the SAME stitches as before. Not measured on the larger corpus of 85 real pictures (it is not on disk); `trace/selftest.mjs` has 7 new checks (frame found, thick border and filled picture left alone, soft JPEG frame, option off). **A grey the palette missed, and a carried line that was not (2026-11-09, `test.jpg` again).** After the fixes above the tiger's muzzle, eyes and teeth were still bare cloth, and "white is like the cloth, by design" was only half true: a PURE white is right to leave to a cream or white shirt (on a darker shirt it is sewn white: tried on navy), but the pale greys of the chart (L about 67, 2.6 % of the design, 34 CIELAB from the nearest sewn colour) are not white. The 12 colour palette had the page's white (55 % of the flat samples), seven dark colours and no light grey: k-means spends its colours on big areas, so the greys went to the nearest colour, which was the white (the "enclosed white" entry, 5.9 % of the picture, left unsewn). `missingColours` (the rescue for ink that is only thin lines) could not help: it runs only when the palette has room, and it counts a grey as "explained" (a mix of white and a dark). New: `missingNeutral` (`trace/quantize.js`, artwork only, not a person with a region map). It works on blocks of the working picture (at most 500,000, each the mean of its pixels, then blurred 3 x 3: the weave of a chart and the noise of a JPEG are gone). A block is a candidate when it is a neutral (chroma at most `NEUTRAL_CHROMA` 22), more than `NEUTRAL_PAGE_DE` (14) from the page's white and more than `NEUTRAL_FAR` (22) from every palette colour; it counts when at least `NEUTRAL_DENSITY` (70 %) of the 5 x 5 blocks round it are candidates too, so the soft ramp of a blurred edge (a band of three blocks at most) never makes a grey (checked: without that rule a blurred black square on white makes a grey of 0.7 % of the picture). The densest group that holds at least `NEUTRAL_MIN_SHARE` (0.5 %) of the picture is the colour. When the palette is full, k-means is asked again for ONE COLOUR FEWER (the others are found again, so what goes is what costs least) and the grey takes the slot: 12 colours, not a needle more. Measured on `test.jpg` at 100 mm (the flat stitch render against the picture, both blurred 0.3 mm): light features inside the design sewn as a light thread 13.6 % -> 47.1 %, mean CIELAB error 11.86 -> 11.80, very dark pixels sewn dark 83.6 % -> 84.5 %, line precision .905 -> .933, hf structure correlation .793 -> .805, 31,282 -> 31,517 stitches (+0.8 %); a grey forced into the palette by hand did the same (47.6 %). By eye the eye is a grey patch with a green iris, the muzzle a grey patch with its dark stripes and a white highlight line, the teeth grey pieces. Also `CARRY_DE` went from 26 to 18, the distance a detail may have to the thread it is sewn in (`DETAIL_MATCH_DE`): a shape the detail could be sewn in carries it, one it could not does not (at 26 a dark line over a blue-grey patch counted as carried by it): 6 -> 10 detail lines, strict line recall .483 -> .506, +60 stitches. Thirteen other pictures (a badge, a cartoon, a sunset gradient, lettering in four styles, the Kazi logo, a letterhead, a model, and a robot, a mountain and an owl drawn for the check) and four blurred or noisy copies give the SAME stitches as before: the grey is only added where a full palette misses a coherent neutral, which is artwork. `trace/selftest.mjs` has 7 more checks (137): the woven grey among 14 colour blocks is found and a slot is made, nothing is missing once it is in the palette, under half a per cent is not worth a colour, a picture without grey has none, a blurred edge is not a grey, the numbers. **A person in a photograph (`trace/regions.js`, `trace/regionPalette.js`, `digitizer/segment.js`, `segmentClient.js`).** A portrait made into flat patches of one shared palette lost its hair into a grey wall (grey hair is 10 away from a grey wall in colour, white hair and a white shirt 8 to 10 from the cream cloth), spent its colours on a flag, and had too few tones in the face. So the page looks for the person first. `segmentClient.js` runs Google's MediaPipe selfie multiclass model (Apache-2.0, 16 MB, kept in `src/assets/models/`, see its README) in a Web Worker, and `segment.js` sharpens its 256 pixel answer to the picture's own edges with a colour-guided filter (about 3 s a picture on a slow laptop, the first time about 8 s with the 30 MB download, which the browser then keeps). The answer is a region map, one class per pixel (0 background, 1 hair, 2 body skin, 3 face skin, 4 clothes, 5 other), given to the tracer as the option `regions`. The page asks only when the first reading says "photo" and the person has not unticked "Find the person in a photo" or "Leave out the background"; a picture with no face (the biggest face-skin piece under 1.5 % of it, or the model not sure of it: the 6 animals and flowers of the check came out at 0.7 % and 0.55 or less, the 20 people at 2.7 % and 0.88 or more), a picture with transparency of its own, a failed load or no Worker is traced as it always was. With a map the tracer (1) makes the background see-through (`cutOutPerson`), so the transparent-logo path leaves it out, the cloth is not taken from it and the design is made as big as the hoop allows for the person alone; (2) builds the palette region by region (`buildRegionalPalette`: each region gets the colours it earns, shared out by how much error one more colour takes away there, weighted by `DEFAULT_REGION_WEIGHTS`; colours of different regions that are alike are one thread) and gives every pixel the nearest colour among its own region's, or those of a region within 0.5 mm (`classifyByRegion`), so grey hair is never given a skin tone and a cheek never a grey of the hair; (3) keeps `reserveColors` (1) of the colours back for the details' own thread, because with all twelve in the picture a face's lids, lashes and brows had no dark thread to be sewn in (`fine.js` found 40 to 70 lines on a portrait and `fineObjects` kept 1 to 8); and (4) tells every fine line and dot which region it lies in, so that threads and stitches go to a face before the pattern of a tie (`DETAIL_REGION_WEIGHTS`). Without a map a photograph is read as before, with two repairs: a colour is only "like the cloth" when it is within `CLOTH_MATCH_PHOTO` (7; never against the default cream, which is only a guess), and a colour near the backdrop's counts as backdrop only when it makes up 1 % of the picture's border ring (`BG_RING_MIN`: hair is not on the edge, the wall is). (5) reads a person half a step higher up the Detail slider (as `detail.js` does for a portrait; on 8 portraits 24 % more shapes for 3 % more stitches, the structure match inside the head 0.691 to 0.698). On 20 portraits (free pictures from Wikimedia Commons, 92 mm tall) against the converter before: stitches 14,349 to 8,660 (-40 %), thread cuts 241 to 137, colour changes 12 to 11, and by eye the wall and the flag are gone, the hair has its own colours and the lashes and brows are sewn in a dark thread. Over the inside of the head (its rim left out, because there the cut-out meets cloth and the photo meets wall) the colour error is 8.55 before and 8.47 after and the structure match (correlation of the gradients of the blurred picture and of the blurred stitches) 0.733 and 0.723: no better and no worse, the face still has only three to five tones. The gain is the hair, the missing half-faces (a pale cheek next to a white wall was taken for the wall), the colours no longer spent on the background, and the stitches it no longer needs. What is still weak: the tones of a face change in steps (soft shading by bands of sparse rows was tried and looked like scribbles, +30 % stitches), eyes are a dark mark or a line (the iris and the white are not found), hair is a flat patch with a few strands, a face under 1.5 % of the picture is not found, and nothing has been sewn on cloth. Other tools tried and left out: vtracer (Node only, and at 12 colours it merges white hair into skin), Coherent Line Drawing and XDoG as the finder of fine lines (the same lines at 2 to 9 times the time).

**Test pictures.** `sample images/` at the repo root holds the owner's own pictures; it is untracked on purpose and must never be committed (the repo is public and the pictures are not ours). Tests use made-up pictures. For judging quality there is nothing in the repo: pictures that are free to use (public domain or Creative Commons, from Wikimedia Commons) and pictures drawn in code are put in a scratch folder outside it, run through the same path the page uses (decode, trace, fit to the hoop, stitches, DST written and read back), drawn beside the source and measured (colour distance and structure similarity after a small blur, since nobody judges a design by single threads).

**Guard rails (so a damaged file or a typo cannot hang the page).** Every number a shape carries (row spacing, stitch length, pull compensation, outline width and length ...) is held to a range the engine can work with, a ring with a NaN or a point further than 5000 mm from the hoop's middle is left out with a warning, and a design that would need more than `maxStitches` (1.5 million) is refused at once with a plain message before any work is done. The page's `engineClient.js` adds a second net: one job per kind in flight, and a job running over two minutes is given up on (the worker that has it is stopped and a fresh one starts; the page has two workers, one for stitches and one for reading pictures, and one stopping leaves the other's jobs alone). Checked in `stitch/selftest.mjs` (damaged settings, a 6000 mm rectangle) and, for the client, with a pretend worker.

**Speed.** A traced portrait has thousands of shapes, so nothing may look at every pair of them. Which shapes lie over which is found by drawing the shapes into one grid (`overlapPairs` in `stitch/engine.js`; `stitch/overlap.js` finds the same pairs on the shapes' own grid for a design too wide for it), and the overlap under later shapes (`bleedUnder` in `digitizer/convert.js`) cuts the neighbours of each shape to the box round it first (`clipToBox`) and, for a big shape cut by many holes, works its edge ring by ring. `nearPairs` (`geometry/poly.js`) and `prepareShape` / `intersectPrepared` (`geometry/clip.js`) are the other way to the same answer (neighbours found on a grid along the outlines, both shapes cut to their shared box); they are kept, tested in `stitch/selftest.mjs`, and not used by the page: on the 129 pictures at 100 mm the stitch engine as it is takes 2.3 s on average and 7.3 s at worst, and the version that used them instead (before the two were put together) 2.7 s and 19.2 s. A 3,500-shape portrait went from about 3 minutes to 45 seconds with the same stitches; the app gives up on any one job after two minutes.

**Known limits.** A thread is 0.4 mm wide, so a line cannot be thinner than about half a millimetre: a source line thinner than that is sewn too heavy, which is why the share of lines falls with the size; lettering under about 4 mm tall loses the counters of its a, e and o. Satin at junctions of non-perpendicular strokes (K, M, X, the points of a star, an A whose bend fans the stitches out more than 2 mm) is tangled or falls back to fill; fine serif lettering under about 10 mm falls back to tatami; lettering has many trims; underlay is by rule of thumb (no fabric-specific testing yet); nothing here has been sewn on a real machine. Pull compensation and density defaults are the usual starting points (`stitch/presets.js`), to be tuned with the factory. The fine details are lines of one thread (0.4 to 0.5 mm) laid on the colour layer: over a dense fill a single thread can sink, and only a sew-out will show how much (the page offers thicker lines); they look at lightness only, so a line of the same lightness as its surroundings but another colour is missed; a picture read as a logo (some greyscale portraits are) gets none; in hair, skin and foliage some of the weaker lines are texture and not detail; each separate line may cost a trim (the strongest and longest are kept, and near ones are chained with jumps).

**Thin lines as lines, and what is left of the dark tones (2026-11-09, `test.jpg` again).** *The whiskers.* The tiger's whiskers are seven lines of one pixel (0.15 mm) that leave the same point a few pixels apart. The tracer kept them as thin features and widened each to three or four pixels (`thickenKept`), the gaps of a pixel or two between them were then closed as slivers, and the fan became ONE shape of 11.75 mm^2 (mean width 0.76 mm, 1.9 mm at the root) that the engine filled as tatami (a strip under 0.7 mm is a line, a wider one is filled and sewn 1.2 to 1.4 mm across: a 0.8 mm strip renders 1.23 mm, a 1 mm one 1.40 mm); the lines the finder had found along them were dropped as "carried" by the blob. `trace/hairlines.js` takes the pieces of the contrast class that are this thin out of the label map BEFORE they are widened (`HAIRLINE`: at most 0.36 mm across, a piece at least 2 mm long, chains of at least 0.8 mm, at most 12 mm of line to a cm^2 of picture and 150 chains, longest first; artwork and photographs, not logos, and only with `details`, option `hairlines`) and returns their centre lines (Zhang-Suen thinning of the piece, followed from end to end, simplified to 0.5 px; a closed ring stays one line; the tangle where lines meet is dropped). The pixels go to the colour beside them. `fine.js` counts the lines as carriers (a finder line along one is not added twice). The converter (`fineObjects`) sews each as one line of bean stitch (0.45 mm, the thinnest line a needle makes), on top, in a thread of the design that is within `HAIRLINE_MATCH_DE` (24) of the colour the line has in the picture (the darkest or lightest half of its pixels, not the palette colour it was drawn in: the whiskers are near black and the nearest palette colour was a slate blue) and shows on what lies under it (`HAIRLINE_VISIBLE_DE`, 8); failing that in the thread of the colour it was drawn in; failing that it is left out. At most `HAIRLINE_THREADS` (2) threads for all of them, each worth 3 mm of line; they join the details' own threads where the colour is the same, and never bring a new colour. Measured at 100 mm: the tiger's fan is a fan of six or seven separate thread lines in the dark thread instead of the blob and one strip; of the picture's thin dark lines (0.6 mm or less across) the share of thread bands over 0.7 mm falls 29.4 to 25.9 % and over 1 mm 14.8 to 12.7 % (median band 0.49 to 0.47 mm), 31,517 to 31,556 stitches and one more colour change (the dark thread comes after the white details). Elsewhere: a bare tree on a sky (a photograph, 49 hairlines) goes from broken pale-grey twigs and a pale blue trunk to a dark brown tree, with 5.6 % fewer stitches, 27 % fewer thread cuts and 26 shapes instead of 64; a furry texture (art, 23 hairlines) 3 % fewer stitches; a photograph of a person, line match F1 .704 to .778 (recall .728 to .827, precision .683 to .734), colour error 5.75 to 5.65, and 2 more colour changes; the 12 other test pictures (logos, drawn art with no thin line) give the same stitches. A first version sewed every hairline in the one dark thread the details had, which put dark lines on a pale dress: a hairline is now only ever sewn in a thread near its own colour (`trace/selftest.mjs`: 15 more checks, 152, among them that a pale line is not sewn in a dark thread and that at most two threads are used). *The dark tones.* The claim that the darkest tones are about 17 L lighter than the picture was wrong in size: it came from looking only at the picture's darkest pixels one by one (they are always lighter after any colour reduction; the thread over the source's pixels of L under 22 has mean L 33 against 16). Seen as the picture is seen (both blurred 0.3 mm) the stitches are 1.5 L lighter on average, 3.6 at the darkest 5 % (18.3 to 21.9) and 2.0 at the darkest 10 %; by band, the source's L 10 to 20 (8 % of the design) is sewn 8.4 lighter, L 20 to 30 (21 %) 5.6 lighter, L 30 to 40 2.2 lighter, L 50 to 60 3.1 darker: the tones are squeezed towards the middle. It is not the palette's average colours (taking them from the whole region instead of its deep inside changed the darkest 5 % by +0.3, the wrong way), and a true black does not help here: the chart's black cells are 0.9 % of the picture, a black thread costs one mid colour of the 12 (tried by hand: colour error 11.80 to 11.70, but dark pixels sewn dark 84.5 to 80.4 % and strict line recall .506 to .437, and by eye no clearer), and 15 colours instead of 12 gave 33.9 (not 32.8): the rest is the small dark marks (one or two chart cells) that the clean-up merges into their neighbours, which the finer slider gets back (Detail 1 for 0.75: 31,556 to 34,534 stitches, the thread over the dark pixels L 32.9 to 30.5, strict line recall .502 to .634, one more colour change). What did get darker is the lines: a whisker, a hair or a twig is now sewn in the colour it has in the picture where the design has a thread near it (the tree's trunk from pale blue-grey to brown-black), not in the palette colour it happened to be drawn in.
